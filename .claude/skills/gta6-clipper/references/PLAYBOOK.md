# The clipping playbook

Researched November 2026. Numbers are what clippers and platforms reported, with the source next to each; many come
from clipping-tool companies, which want clipping to look good. Treat them as a rough guide, never a promise. Rules
and rates change: check the campaign page or platform help page before relying on one.

## Part 1. How clipping works and pays

A clipper cuts short vertical clips from someone else's long content (streams, podcasts, YouTube videos), posts them
on accounts the clipper owns, and gets paid by the views. It is big: in the 30 days to 26 Aug 2026, 1,820 accounts
posted 9,941 tagged clips of just six streamers (IShowSpeed, Kai Cenat, N3on, Adin Ross, xQc, CaseOh), for 1.68
billion YouTube views and 260 million TikTok plays (creatordb.app/creator-news/the-rise-of-the-clip-economy).

**Four ways to get paid**

1. **Clipping campaigns on a marketplace (the usual way to start).** A streamer or brand puts up a budget and a rate per
   1,000 views. You post clips, submit the links, and get paid for the views that are checked.
   - **Content Rewards on Whop** is the biggest (how to join, step by step: `WHOP.md`). On 13 Nov 2026 the middle rate across 712 live campaigns was about
     **$1.04 per 1,000 views**; streamer campaigns sat around $1 to $1.50, gaming campaigns around $1.40
     (contentrewards.com).
   - Most campaigns pay nothing until a clip earns a minimum (often $2) and stop at a cap (often $35 to $300 per clip).
   - Content Rewards keeps a 10% fee (a payment-processing fee may be added) and pays about 10 days after approval. Views are counted through the
     platforms, an AI checks each clip against the campaign rules, and suspicious views go to a human (sprites.ai,
     Whop terms of 3 Sep 2026). Plan for 20 to 30% of views never being counted.
   - Others: Vyro (backed by MrBeast; rates vary by campaign), Clipping, Clipping.net. Clip Affiliates has closed.
2. **A streamer's own program** (usually a Discord). Kick streamer N3on reportedly paid about $1.4M to 303 clippers in
   five weeks at $40 to $50 per 100,000 views (Business Insider via Tubefilter, Apr 2026). Never pay to join one and
   never give anyone your account password.
3. **Creator funds.** TikTok Creator Rewards needs 10,000 followers, 100,000 views in 30 days, and original videos of
   **at least 1 minute**; paid campaign clips don't count. YouTube Shorts shares 45% of Shorts ad money with
   creators. Clip channels are judged hard on originality (Part 3).
4. **Clipping as a service.** Editing clips for a streamer: roughly $15 to $55 a clip, or a monthly retainer (agency
   price guides). A clip page with followers can also be sold later.

**What people really make (self-reported):** a typical beginner clip gets a few hundred views. Beginners make about
$100 to $500 a month; consistent clippers $500 to $2,000; the top few $5,000 to $15,000 after 6+ months of daily
posting. Emrah Bayraktar's first payout was $12; two weeks later, $2,500 (NPR, May 2026). Eknoor Singh says he makes
at least £10,000 a month; his first payout was $300 from one clip (BBC, Oct 2026). At about $1 per 1,000 views,
$100 needs 100,000 paid views.

## Part 2. Starting a clipping channel, step by step

1. **Pick the streamer.**
   - Pick someone who already goes viral and streams a lot (raw material every day). Bayraktar: "ride waves, don't
     create them"; skip creators who have never had a viral video.
   - Best of all: a streamer with a clipping campaign or program (you get paid and you have permission). Search Whop
     Content Rewards for their name. Look for campaigns at $1+ per 1,000 views with plenty of budget left; small
     campaigns from rising streamers have far fewer clippers to compete with.
   - Read the campaign rules before the first clip: which platforms, credits or tags, length, audio, whether the page
     must be a dedicated fan page. Breaking a rule is the main reason clips get rejected.
   - No campaign? Fan clips are usually welcome, but the streamer owes you nothing and can ask for removal. On Kick,
     streamers can switch clipping off.
2. **One page per streamer.** A clean name with no numbers or symbols (e.g. "Kai Cenat Moments"), the streamer's
   face as the photo, a one-line bio saying why to follow ("Kai's funniest moments, every day"). It should look like a
   fan brand, not a repost bin. Use a fresh email for each set of accounts.
3. **The same page on TikTok, YouTube (Shorts) and Instagram (Reels).** A clip that flops on one often hits on another.
4. **Warm up new accounts for at least 24 hours before the first post.** A few 10-minute sessions: search the
   streamer and the game, watch, like and comment like a normal fan. Accounts that post on day one often get no views
   or get banned (Bayraktar; r/whop).
5. **Post every day.** Bayraktar: 1 to 2 clips a day, at most 6, but every day without fail; quality first. Cole
   Corcoran's volume method: 6 clips a day on 3 platforms. Start with 1 to 3 good ones and add more once your clips
   reliably get views.
6. **Be fast with fresh moments.** The first good clip of a moment usually wins. During a big stream, clip while it is
   happening (`find.py hot` shows what viewers are clipping right now; the live stream's recording can be clipped
   while it is still going).
7. **Judge it monthly, not hourly.** Bayraktar had about 4 months of 100 to 300 views before his first hit.
   Most clipping accounts fail because the owner quits in that stretch.

## Part 3. The rules that can sink a clip channel (2026)

- **YouTube:** clips "with little or no narrative" or "copied without substantive changes" can't earn money, and
  **permission from the streamer does not change that** (YouTube help 1311392). One bad pattern can take
  monetization off the whole channel. On 1 Oct 2026 YouTube said Shorts will reach fewer people for channels that
  mostly re-upload others' videos "without significant changes", and suggested commentary, unique edits or
  storytelling.
- **Instagram:** since about 30 Apr 2026, accounts that mostly post others' content without real changes are not
  recommended to non-followers. Watermarks, small crops, light edits and crediting the creator do **not** count as
  changes; your own words, commentary and remixing do (PetaPixel).
- **TikTok:** unoriginal re-uploads are not shown on the For You feed and don't count for Creator Rewards.
- **What counts as your own edit:** start on the moment, a title hook, captions, short context notes, cutting several
  moments into one story, a unique caption on each upload. This skill does all of these on every clip.
- **Music is the biggest copyright risk.** A song playing on stream is not licensed for your clip. Twitch mutes
  copyrighted music in recordings (the finder warns you). Choose moments without music, or expect a mute or claim.
- **Games:** Rockstar's video policy covers non-commercial fan use; it has tolerated GTA videos for years, but that
  is not formal permission. Never post a full trailer or cutscene without a reaction on top (Take-Two has claimed
  those). On YouTube, graphic game violence in the first 7 seconds costs ads (Sept 2026 rules).
- **Gambling streams** pay more per view but get accounts banned far more often. Keep them off your main pages.
- **Bans happen.** Keep your skills and footage, start a new page if one goes, never share passwords.

## Part 4. Why most clip channels fail

1. Quitting during the slow first months.
2. Posting raw cuts with "lol" captions (about 200 views, and demoted in 2026).
3. Clipping boring moments or creators with no momentum; joining dying campaigns.
4. No warm-up; spammy names and bios.
5. Ignoring the campaign's rules.
6. Posting in bursts, then stopping.
7. Posting to one platform only.
8. Expecting fast money: about $1 per 1,000 paid views, after minimums, caps and fees.
